## Introduction

For a lot of engineers, getting into a top fintech company feels like one of those goals that sits in the background for years. You hear about the scale, the engineering standards, and the kind of systems involved. Then, at some point, you start wondering what it would actually feel like to be inside one. That was [Visa](https://www.visa.co.in/) for me.

This summer, I joined [Visa](https://www.visa.co.in/) as a Systems Architect Intern, expecting an exciting opportunity to learn from engineers working at that scale. The internship had everything I had imagined and more: meaningful work, modern tools, a great intern cohort, workshops, outings, and plenty of moments that made it feel like a full summer experience instead of just another role on a resume.

This blog is my attempt to put that journey into words, from the off-campus selection process to the work, the people, and everything that happened beyond the desk. I will keep technical details at a high level, but I want to share the parts of the experience that genuinely changed how I think about engineering.

![Visa internship portrait](/visa-internship-experience/4.jpeg)

## How I Got This Opportunity

The journey began when I came across an opening at Visa that genuinely interested me. I reached out to a few people through LinkedIn for a referral and was fortunate to receive responses. One of those conversations led to a referral for the role.

The process began with resume shortlisting, followed by an online assessment and then technical and conversational round. These rounds covered DSA, core computer science fundamentals such as computer networks and operating systems, system design, puzzles, and situational and behavioural questions.

I liked that the process did not reward preparation in just one area. It tested problem-solving, fundamentals, communication, and the ability to think through unfamiliar situations without rushing to an answer. There were questions where the final answer mattered, but the reasoning behind it mattered just as much.

Getting the offer felt especially rewarding because it came after a process that challenged me across different dimensions of engineering. It was one of those moments that made all the preparation, practice, and cold messages worth it.

## Joining Visa: First Impressions

The first few days at Visa were a lot to take in. There were new tools, new terms, new people, and a much larger engineering environment than I had worked in before. Still, the place felt less intimidating than I expected.

A big reason was the people. I never felt hesitant about asking questions, and the answers usually came with the context behind them. That made a real difference. As an intern, it is easy to focus on finishing the next task, but understanding why a system or process exists is what helps the work start making sense.

The culture had a good balance to it. There was a high bar for the work, and people cared about doing things properly, but it never felt overly formal. You could have a serious technical discussion, ask a basic question without overthinking it, and then move into a completely unrelated conversation a few minutes later. That made the environment feel both professional and comfortable.

By the end of the first week, I had stopped thinking of it as just a new workplace. It already felt like a place where I could learn quickly, contribute meaningfully, and enjoy the process along the way.

## What I Worked Around

I worked with the DX - Solution Engineering team. The role gave me exposure to solution architecture first, which meant understanding how different technical pieces fit together before jumping into implementation.

My work broadly touched development, infrastructure provisioning, and the engineering practices around them. It was useful to see how a solution is approached in a real organisation: first understand the requirement and constraints, then think through the architecture, and only then decide what needs to be built or configured.

I also got to share a few GenAI tools and techniques with the team, including reusable Skills for common tasks. It was a small but useful part of the internship, and helped me think about how new tools can be introduced in a way that is practical for everyday engineering work.

Since the work involved internal systems, I cannot share more details. But the internship gave me a practical view of how engineering decisions travel beyond code. A solution has to make sense not only when it is first built, but also when someone else has to use it, extend it, or troubleshoot it later.

## What Real Engineering Looked Like

The internship gave me a clearer picture of how engineering work moves from an idea to something that can actually be used. The code is important, but it is only one part of the process. Before implementation, there is understanding the requirement, identifying constraints, thinking through the architecture, and deciding what a sensible solution should look like.

I also saw how much value there is in being deliberate. A solution should not only work for the immediate case. It should be clear enough for another engineer to understand, practical enough to fit the existing environment, and flexible enough to change when requirements do. Thinking through those questions early often saves more time than rushing into implementation.

GenAI was another interesting part of the workflow. I got hands-on exposure to coding agents such as [Claude](https://claude.ai/) and [Codex](https://openai.com/codex/), mainly for exploring unfamiliar areas, comparing approaches, and iterating faster. The important part was learning to use them with intent: provide the context that matters, verify what comes back, and most importantly, optimize token usage.

That was the biggest learning for me. Real engineering is not only about getting something to work. It is about making decisions that hold up beyond the first successful run.

## Intern Life Beyond the Desk

The internship had a life of its own outside the work. There was always something happening with the intern cohort, which made the summer feel more like a full experience than a typical office routine.

We had workshops focused on overall development, a CSR activity supporting a social cause, and an intern outing at an adventure resort. The outing was a highlight. It was a day to step away from the usual work context, have fun, and spend time with people who had quickly gone from being fellow interns to friends.

I also participated in Visa’s Toastmasters sessions, which was a great way to build my communication skills (and I was fortunate to win an Intern Spotlight Award 😁).

The smaller moments made it even better: shared lunches, long conversations, team activities, and a lot of free food. Together, they gave the internship the kind of energy you hope for from a great summer experience: interesting work, good people, and plenty of stories to take back with you.

## How I Prepared

I can only speak from my own process, but the preparation was not about finding one perfect question list. It was about being comfortable enough with the fundamentals to handle different kinds of questions without getting thrown off.

For DSA, practising a range of problems helps build confidence across different topics. Decent practice on [Codeforces](https://codeforces.com/) and [LeetCode](https://leetcode.com/), along with regular contests, helped me a lot. Be able to explain your approach, discuss its complexity, and improve it when an interviewer adds a constraint.

For core CS, learn things from first principles. Instead of only memorising things from computer networks or operating systems or other topics, understand why a concept exists, what problem it solves, and how it behaves in a real system. For system design, practise breaking a problem into requirements, components, data flow, trade-offs, and possible failure points. Puzzles can feel unpredictable, but approaching them calmly and explaining your thinking goes a long way.

Finally, do not treat behavioural and situational questions as an afterthought. Be ready to talk honestly about projects, challenges, teamwork, decisions you have made, and what you learned from them. A strong interview is usually less about sounding perfect and more about showing how you think.

## Key Takeaways and Conclusion

A few things from the summer will stay with me:

- ***Strong engineering starts with fundamentals.*** Whether the task involves architecture, infrastructure, or GenAI, understanding the underlying system makes it easier to make good decisions.
- ***A working solution is only the beginning.*** It also needs to be practical, maintainable, and clear enough for others to work with.
- ***GenAI tools are most useful when they are used with intent.*** Good context, careful review, and efficient token usage matter as much as the tool itself.
- ***Communication is part of engineering.*** Being able to explain an idea clearly, ask the right questions, and discuss trade-offs makes technical work more effective.
- ***The people around you make a real difference.*** A team that is willing to share context, answer questions, and make work enjoyable changes how quickly you learn.

Overall, the internship gave me a more practical understanding of engineering at scale. I came away more interested in solution architecture, infrastructure, and developer experience, but also more aware of the responsibility behind building systems that other people depend on.

Beyond the technical exposure, the internship helped me grow in how I communicate, collaborate, take feedback, and approach unfamiliar problems. Looking back, that overall growth is one of the things I value most about the experience.

A good internship helps in more ways than one. It gives you room to learn, exposes you to what real engineering looks like, and helps you understand the kind of work you want to do next. It can also open doors, whether that means earning a PPO at the same organisation or carrying meaningful work and a strong name on your resume into future opportunities.

For me, this was a summer full of learning, work, and memorable experiences. I am grateful to everyone who made it possible.
