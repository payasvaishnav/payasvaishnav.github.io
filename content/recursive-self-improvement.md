Think about learning to program. At first, the process looks simple. You read tutorials, solve problems, build small projects, and gradually become better at writing code.

That is ordinary improvement. You spend effort on a skill and become more capable at it.

But after a while, you may notice that the way you are learning is inefficient. Maybe passive reading does not stick. Maybe you learn more from building projects, testing yourself, reviewing mistakes, or explaining an idea to someone else. At that point, you are no longer only improving at programming. You are improving **the process that makes you better** at programming.

That small shift is the starting point for recursive self-improvement, or ***RSI***.

## Where the recursion begins

Finding a better way to learn gives you a useful boost, but that alone does not make the process recursive. You may discover that building projects teaches you more than reading tutorials. Your learning improves, but the change could still be a one-time adjustment.

The loop begins when the improved learner becomes better at finding the next adjustment. Better practice gives you more experience. More experience makes it easier to notice patterns in your mistakes. Those patterns help you change how you practice again.

Now each improvement does two things. It helps you learn programming today, and it improves your ability to make the next learning decision. The output of the process begins to improve the process itself.

That is what makes RSI different from ordinary improvement.

## A feedback loop is not an explosion

The word *recursive* makes people imagine an unstoppable curve. A system gets better, then gets better at getting better, then suddenly improves faster and faster.

That can happen in principle, but it does not follow from the existence of a feedback loop.

The modern intuition behind an [intelligence explosion](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0065-2458(08)60418-0) is often traced to statistician I. J. Good. In 1965, he argued that a machine capable of designing intelligent machines could design a better one. That possibility is important, but it is still conditional. It describes what could happen if the loop becomes strong enough, not what every feedback loop must do.

Imagine that a better learning system helps you improve twenty percent faster. You use that advantage to find another improvement, but it only helps by five percent. The next change might help by one percent. The feedback loop is still working. Each improvement is helping produce the next one. The gains are simply showing diminishing returns.

This is the distinction that matters. RSI describes the **feedback mechanism**. It does not tell us the **growth rate**.

A loop can accelerate. It can slow down. It can eventually stop making meaningful progress. The actual dynamics depend on what the system is trying to improve and what limits it runs into along the way.

```text
Accelerating returns
1x → 2x → 4x → 8x → ...

Diminishing returns
1x → 1.04x → 1.06x → 1.07x → ...
```

## Every loop has bottlenecks

Once we separate recursion from explosion, the next question becomes more practical. What determines how quickly a feedback loop can run?

In engineering, an improvement loop needs more than a good idea. It needs a way to make a change, observe the result, decide whether the change helped, and try again. The slowest part of that cycle becomes a **bottleneck**.

For someone learning programming, the bottleneck might be time, attention, or useful feedback. You can improve your study system, but you still need enough projects to expose mistakes. You still need time to reflect on them. A better process helps, but it cannot remove every constraint.

This matters because feedback loops have **latency**. If it takes a long time to observe whether a change worked, the next iteration has to wait. If feedback is noisy, the system may not know which change actually helped. If the resources required to test an idea are limited, progress slows even when the underlying reasoning is strong.

RSI is therefore not a machine running in empty space. It is an optimization process operating inside a real system with real constraints.

## Why AI changes the picture

RSI has become a popular term in AI discussions because people see the possibility of AI helping build better AI. But the idea itself is broader than that.

Human self-improvement is mostly indirect. We change our habits, tools, environment, and methods, but we cannot inspect our own brains the way an engineer inspects a system.

AI introduces a different possibility. An AI system can help with some of the work involved in building better AI systems. It can write code, analyze results, search through information, generate hypotheses, and evaluate possible changes. Those are not the entire AI development process, but they are parts of it.

This creates an interesting loop. People build an AI system. That system helps people do some of the work required to build the next system. The next system may then be more useful in that same work.

The important point is that this does not require an AI to sit alone and rewrite its own source code. The system doing the improving can be much larger. It can include people, models, data, compute, experiments, and the infrastructure needed to run them.

That is why the question is not simply whether AI has achieved RSI. A better question is how much of the process that creates better intelligence can intelligence itself take part in.

## The system around the thinker

Human self-improvement is indirect, but that does not mean it happens only inside the brain. A large part of human intelligence has always lived outside the person doing the thinking.

A notebook can preserve an idea that would otherwise be forgotten. A calculator can handle steps that would be slow and error-prone by hand. A search engine can retrieve information that no individual could keep in memory. These tools do not change the biological brain, but they change the effective capability of the person using them.

From an engineering perspective, the useful system is often larger than the thinker. It includes memory, tools, feedback, and the environment in which decisions are made. A programmer with a debugger, documentation, tests, and version control is working with a much stronger cognitive system than a programmer with only a text editor.

This matters for RSI because external tools can improve the process of improvement itself. A good note-taking system can help you learn better. Better learning can help you design a better note-taking system. The loop is no longer contained inside one brain.

Once we see that, the meaning of “self” starts to become less obvious. The self-improving unit may be a person together with the systems that extend their ability to think.

## When improvements survive their creators

The important property of **cognitive infrastructure** is not only that it helps one person think better. It can preserve an improvement after the person who made it is gone.

Writing changed the structure of human learning. A useful idea no longer had to survive only through memory or conversation. It could be recorded, copied, tested, criticized, and used by people who were never in the same room as its original author.

This created cumulative capability. A student learning calculus does not need to rediscover calculus. An engineer using a library does not need to rebuild every technique that made the library possible. Each generation can begin from a more capable starting point.

Science is a good example of this feedback loop. Better theories lead to better instruments. Better instruments produce better observations. Better observations reveal problems with existing theories and create the conditions for better ones.

Civilization is not a single mind, but its cognitive infrastructure can behave like a distributed system for producing knowledge. People, tools, institutions, records, and infrastructure all carry part of the state. The system improves when an advance in one part makes the other parts more capable.

At that scale, RSI is no longer only about an individual becoming better. It becomes a process through which a civilization improves its ability to create knowledge, tools, and future improvements.

## AI inside the loop

AI fits into this story because it is not only a tool for storing or retrieving knowledge. It can take part in some of the cognitive work that produces new knowledge and new tools.

Writing preserved ideas. Computers made it possible to execute complex procedures at scale. AI can help reason through information, generate code, inspect results, and explore possible solutions. In some cases, those tasks are themselves part of the process of building better software, better research tools, and better AI systems.

This makes AI a new kind of component in our cognitive infrastructure. It is not simply an archive or a calculator. It can participate in the iteration cycle.

The relevant system is therefore not just an AI model. It is people using models, models operating on tools, tools producing data, and institutions deciding what gets tested and deployed. The loop remains distributed, but some of its cognitive work can now be delegated to the system being improved.

That is the important transition. The question is not whether a machine suddenly becomes a fully autonomous self-improver. The question is how much of the improvement loop can gradually move from people into the cognitive infrastructure they have built.

If that loop becomes substantially tighter, the potential is enormous. It could help accelerate scientific discovery, engineering, medicine, and the creation of new cognitive tools. The same capability could also create serious risks if development moves faster than our ability to evaluate, govern, and control powerful systems. Neither outcome is inevitable, but RSI changes both what becomes possible and how quickly it may become possible.

## The question worth asking

RSI is easy to misunderstand when it is treated as a magical property of a machine. It is better understood as a feedback loop in which improvements make future improvements easier to find, test, or apply.

Humans have been building versions of this loop for a long time. We improved learning through education, preserved knowledge through writing, improved discovery through science, and built cognitive infrastructure that allowed later generations to start from a stronger position.

AI matters because it may become an active part of that infrastructure. It can increasingly participate in some of the work that produces the next generation of tools and systems. Whether that produces gradual progress, diminishing returns, or rapid acceleration depends on the bottlenecks that remain outside the loop.

So the useful question is not whether RSI has arrived as a single event. It is how much of the process that creates better intelligence can be carried out by intelligence itself.

The answer may change slowly. But if that fraction keeps increasing, the system we are watching may not be one machine improving alone. It may be civilization learning to improve its own cognitive infrastructure.

**The important shift is not a machine becoming magical. It is intelligence becoming a more active part of the system that improves intelligence.**
